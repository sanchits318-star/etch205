Section 02 • Social Sustainability Literature

Critical Analysis of Two Picture Books

Examining cultural belonging, migration narratives, and relational equity in early childhood literature.

Book 01

My Two Blankets

By Irena Kobald & Freya Blackwood (2014) • Little Hare Books

Core Discourse: Cultural Diversity, Migration & Belonging
Visual Metaphor: Cold waterfall vs. dual-coloured woven blanket
Child Agency: Peer-initiated non-verbal hospitality
Lived Link: Navigating language barriers & new spaces

The picture book My Two Blankets by Kobald and Blackwood (2014) explores the core social sustainability discourse of cultural diversity, migration, social justice, and belonging. Boldermo and Odegaard (2019) emphasize that social sustainability requires early learning spaces to actively support migrant children's sense of belonging and cultural identity. The narrative follows Cartwheel, a young refugee girl who arrives in a new country feeling overwhelmed by a cold, alienating waterfall of strange sounds. Through the warmth of her original language blanket, Cartwheel maintains emotional safety until a local girl reaches out to build an intercultural friendship.

Kobald and Blackwood (2014) position children as active agents of social change through the small, responsive gestures of the local friend, who uses smiles, swings, and shared words to bridge cultural divides. As Weldemariam et al. (2022) observe, care and relational ethics are central to social sustainability, empowering young children to initiate inclusive practices without relying on adult direction. Freya Blackwood's illustrations visually reinforce this transformation, depicting Cartwheel's movement from a thin, isolated blanket to weaving a vibrant, dual-colored blanket that honors both her heritage and her new community connections.

Connecting story themes to children's lived experiences allows young learners to process feelings of displacement, language barriers, and peer acceptance. Sneddon and Pettit (2024) contend that sharing migration narratives helps children recognize their social handprint, encouraging them to welcome peers who may feel overwhelmed in unfamiliar learning environments. By discussing Cartwheel's journey, early childhood educators can facilitate deep conversations about hospitality, non-verbal communication, and honoring linguistic diversity.

Book 02

The Invisible Boy

By Trudy Ludwig & Patrice Barton (2013) • Alfred A. Knopf

Core Discourse: Relational Equity, Peer Inclusion & Anti-Exclusion
Visual Metaphor: Grayscale isolation shifting to radiant full colour
Child Agency: Reciprocal kindness & cultural food validation
Lived Link: Group selection, lunchtimes & quiet peers

In The Invisible Boy, Ludwig and Barton (2013) address the social sustainability theme of relational equity, peer inclusion, and anti-exclusion within everyday classroom communities. Brown (2017) asserts that tackling social justice in early childhood requires addressing quiet forms of peer exclusion and ensuring all children receive equitable recognition and respect. The story depicts Brian, a quiet boy who is consistently overlooked by peers during kickball team selection and birthday party invitations, illustrating how subtle classroom dynamics can marginalize quiet children.

Children are positioned as transformational change agents when a new student, Justin, arrives and Brian reaches out with a small note of kindness validating Justin's cultural food, bulgogi. Ludwig and Barton (2013) demonstrate how Justin reciprocates by inviting Brian into a collaborative group project, effectively dismantling Brian's social invisibility. Magiropoulos and Giugni (2007) argue that everyday sustainability is enacted through these micro-moments of peer empathy and collaborative problem-solving. Visually, Barton's illustrations track Brian's emotional shift from muted grayscale tones to radiant full color as he experiences meaningful social inclusion.

The events in The Invisible Boy directly connect to children's daily lived experiences of group play, lunchroom interactions, and navigating peer dynamics in early childhood settings. As Hawkins (2014) highlights, critical pedagogical discussions around literature prompt children to reflect on fairness, notice left-out peers, and actively construct inclusive play environments. Through this story, children learn that small, intentional acts of welcoming can significantly transform classroom culture and build sustainable social communities.
