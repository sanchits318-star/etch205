Section 02 • Social Sustainability Literature

Critical Analysis of Two Picture Books

Examining cultural belonging, migration narratives and relational equity in early childhood literature.

Book 01

My Two Blankets

By Irena Kobald, illustrated by Freya Blackwood (2014) • Little Hare Books

Core Discourse: Cultural Diversity, Migration & Belonging
Visual Metaphor: Cold waterfall versus dual-coloured woven blanket
Child Agency: Peer-initiated non-verbal hospitality
Lived Link: Navigating language barriers and unfamiliar spaces

The picture book My Two Blankets by Kobald and Blackwood (2014) explores cultural diversity, migration, social justice and belonging as dimensions of social sustainability. Boldermo and Ødegaard (2019) explain that social sustainability in early childhood must consider migrant children's experiences and their opportunities to develop a sense of belonging. The narrative follows Cartwheel, a young girl who arrives in a new country feeling overwhelmed by a cold, alienating waterfall of unfamiliar sounds. Her original language blanket provides emotional security while she adjusts to her new surroundings and begins to develop a friendship with a local girl.

Kobald and Blackwood (2014) position children as active contributors to social inclusion through the small, responsive gestures of Cartwheel's new friend. Smiles, shared activities and opportunities to communicate help bridge cultural and linguistic differences. Weldemariam et al. (2022) identify care and relational ethics as important dimensions of social sustainability in early childhood education. Freya Blackwood's illustrations reinforce this transformation by depicting Cartwheel's movement from an isolated blanket towards a vibrant, dual-coloured blanket that represents her original identity and new community connections.

Connecting the story to children's lived experiences allows young learners to consider feelings associated with displacement, language barriers and peer acceptance. Sneddon and Pettit (2024) discuss the importance of diversity, inclusion and community relationships within sustainable early childhood settings. Through Cartwheel's journey, educators can facilitate conversations about hospitality, non-verbal communication and respect for linguistic diversity. The narrative therefore offers opportunities for children to recognise how welcoming actions contribute to a socially sustainable learning community.

Book 02

The Invisible Boy

By Trudy Ludwig, illustrated by Patrice Barton (2013) • Alfred A. Knopf

Core Discourse: Relational Equity, Peer Inclusion & Anti-Exclusion
Visual Metaphor: Grayscale isolation shifting to radiant full colour
Child Agency: Reciprocal kindness and cultural food validation
Lived Link: Group selection, lunchtime interactions and quiet peers

In The Invisible Boy, Ludwig and Barton (2013) address relational equity, peer inclusion and exclusion within everyday classroom communities. Hawkins (2014) highlights the importance of early childhood education in developing children's understandings of difference, diversity, equity and social justice. The story depicts Brian, a quiet boy who is frequently overlooked by peers during kickball team selection and birthday party invitations. These everyday interactions illustrate how subtle classroom dynamics can marginalise children and affect their sense of belonging.

Children are positioned as contributors to social change when Brian reaches out to Justin, a new student, through a small act of kindness that acknowledges his cultural food, bulgogi. Ludwig and Barton (2013) show how Justin reciprocates by inviting Brian into a collaborative group project. This interaction challenges Brian's social invisibility and demonstrates how peer relationships can create opportunities for inclusion. Giugni and Magiropoulos (2007) also draw attention to the relationship between equity and everyday sustainability in early childhood settings. Visually, Barton's illustrations trace Brian's emotional shift from muted grayscale tones towards full colour as he experiences meaningful social connection.

The events in The Invisible Boy connect with children's daily experiences of group play, lunchtime interactions and peer relationships. Hawkins (2014) emphasises the role of early childhood educators in supporting positive attitudes towards diversity and justice. Through this story, children can reflect on fairness, recognise peers who may feel excluded and identify practical ways to create inclusive play environments. Small, intentional acts of kindness can therefore contribute to stronger relationships and more sustainable classroom communities.
